Section 1Basics of AI and ML

1.1 · What is AI?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the field of building systems that perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence — perceiving, reasoning, learning, planning, understanding language, and making decisions.

Most modern engineering uses the rational agent view: an agent perceives its environment through sensors and acts through actuators to maximize its expected performance. Two axes help frame "intelligence": thinking vs. acting (does it reason like a human, or just behave usefully?) and human-like vs. rational (does it imitate humans, or act optimally toward a goal?).

The two great families of AI

Security Lens — why the definition matters

A symbolic system's logic can be reviewed like source code. A learned model's "logic" lives in millions/billions of numeric weights that cannot be directly inspected. This opacity is the root cause of most AI-specific risk: you often can't tell why a model decided something, whether it memorized sensitive data, or whether an attacker nudged it. Your threat model shifts from "review the code" to "reason about data, training, and inference behavior."

1.2 · History and Evolution of AI

The story to be able to tell: philosophical foundations (1943–56) named the field at Dartmouth; early optimism around the Perceptron cooled when its limits (the XOR problem) were exposed, triggering the first winter; rule-based expert systems over-promised into a second winter; the field then shifted from hand-coded rules to learning from data; AlexNet (2012) proved deep networks + big data + GPUs was the winning formula; and the Transformer (2017) unlocked the LLM/generative wave.

Security Lens — the tech always outran its defenses

Expert systems introduced tamperable rule bases; statistical ML introduced data poisoning and adversarial inputs; LLMs/RAG introduced prompt injection and training-data extraction. Security has trailed capability at every step — you're catching up on a decade of accumulated, under-defended surface.

1.3 · Key Concepts in AI

  • Agent & environment — anything that perceives (sensors) and acts (actuators). Described with PEAS: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors.
  • Rationality — acting to maximize expected performance given what the agent knows (not omniscience).
  • Search & optimization — exploring possible states/solutions efficiently, often with heuristics.
  • Knowledge representation & reasoning — how facts are stored and how new conclusions are inferred.
  • Learning — improving performance from data/experience (the ML core).
  • Uncertainty — real-world agents reason probabilistically (Bayesian methods).

Section 2Types of AI

There are two orthogonal ways to classify AI. Don't confuse them: one is about how general the system is; the other is about how it learns.

2.1 · Narrow AI vs. General AI

Narrow AI (ANI)General AI (AGI)Super AI (ASI)
ScopeOne specific task/domainAny task a human can doSurpasses humans broadly
StatusEverything that exists todayHypothetical / research goalHypothetical
ExamplesSpam filters, image classifiers, ChatGPT, AlphaGo——
Security Lens

Nearly all operational risk is narrow-AI risk, here and now — poisoning, evasion, prompt injection, data leakage. Keep threat modeling grounded in deployed systems; AGI-alignment debates are a different problem class than defending this quarter's production model.

2.2 · Supervised Learning

Learn a mapping from inputs X to outputs Y using labeled examples, then generalize to unseen inputs.

Sub-typeOutputExample tasksAlgorithms
ClassificationDiscrete classSpam vs. not-spam, malware family, image labelLogistic regression, SVM, trees, random forest, gradient boosting, neural nets
RegressionContinuous valuePrice, risk score, latencyLinear regression, regression trees, neural nets
Security Lens

Supervised learning depends entirely on label integrity. Corrupt the labels or inject mislabeled samples (data poisoning) and the model learns the attacker's intended behavior — e.g., a poisoned training set teaching a malware classifier that a specific attacker tool is "benign."

2.3 · Unsupervised Learning

Find structure in unlabeled data — patterns, groupings, or compressed representations discovered on its own.

  • Clustering — group similar items (k-means, hierarchical, DBSCAN). Log clustering, user segments.
  • Dimensionality reduction — compress many features into a few (PCA, t-SNE/UMAP, autoencoders).
  • Anomaly detection — flag data points that don't fit learned "normal."
Security Lens — your home turf

Unsupervised anomaly detection underpins UEBA, beaconing detection, and endpoint/DNS outlier hunting. Two things to internalize: (1) "normal" is defined by training data, so baseline poisoning lets an attacker slowly drift the baseline until malicious activity looks normal (boiling-frog); (2) the real defensive challenge is precision, not just recall — low precision buries the SOC.

2.4 · Reinforcement Learning (RL)

An agent learns by interacting with an environment, taking actions, and receiving rewards. The goal is a policy that maximizes cumulative long-term reward — learning by trial and error, not from a fixed labeled set. Formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

The central tension is exploration vs. exploitation: try new actions to find better rewards, or exploit known-good ones. You'll meet RL in game-playing (AlphaGo), robotics, control, and RLHF for LLMs.

Security Lens

Reward hacking: an agent exploits a poorly specified reward to score high without the intended behavior. For LLMs, manipulating the feedback/reward signal (or the labelers behind it) is an alignment attack surface. Agentic AI that takes actions in real systems can be steered into harmful actions if its objective or observations are tampered with.

2.5 · Natural Language Processing (NLP)

Enabling machines to understand and generate human language.

Security Lens

NLP/LLM systems are where prompt injection lives — the top LLM-specific risk. Because these systems don't cleanly separate instructions from data, attacker-controlled text (a user message, a web page, a retrieved document, an email) can hijack behavior. Also relevant: sensitive-information disclosure and jailbreaks.

2.6 · Computer Vision (CV)

Enabling machines to interpret visual data.

TaskOutput
Image classificationOne label for the whole image
Object detectionBounding boxes + labels per object
Semantic segmentationA class label for every pixel
Instance segmentationPixel masks per individual object

Historically powered by CNNs (Section 6.3); increasingly by Vision Transformers (ViT).

Security Lens

CV is the classic stage for adversarial examples: a few crafted pixels (imperceptible to humans) flip a classifier's output. Physical variants — a sticker on a stop sign — matter for camera/vision products. Deepfakes and synthetic media (a generative-CV problem) feed phishing, fraud, and identity attacks.

Section 3Core Components of AI Systems

Every AI system rests on three interdependent pillars. Weakness in any one degrades the whole — and each is a distinct attack surface.

3.1 · Algorithms and Models

Keep these distinct — people conflate them constantly:

  • Algorithm = the procedure/recipe for learning (gradient descent, decision-tree induction, backpropagation). Fixed and general.
  • Model = the learned artifact produced by running an algorithm on data. A model = architecture (structure) + parameters/weights (learned numbers).
Algorithm + Data + Compute ──train──► Model (architecture + weights)
Model + New input ──infer──► Prediction
Security Lens

The trained model is an asset to protect. Threats: model theft/extraction (query an API enough to clone it), model tampering (alter weights, embed a backdoor), and supply-chain risk — a pretrained model or checkpoint pulled from a public hub can be maliciously modified/trojaned. Treat model artifacts like signed binaries: provenance, integrity checks, a vetted registry.

3.2 · Data

Data is the fuel, and data quality dominates model quality ("garbage in, garbage out").

DimensionWhy it matters
QuantityDeep models need large datasets to generalize
QualityErrors, noise, and mislabels propagate into the model
RepresentativenessUnrepresentative data → bias and poor real-world performance
LabelingSupervised learning depends on correct labels
SplitsTrain / validation / test must not leak into each other
DriftReal-world data shifts over time → model decay
Security Lens

Data is the richest attack surface in ML: training-data poisoning, label flipping, backdoor triggers (a hidden pattern forcing a target output), and privacy attacks (reconstruct data or infer membership from a trained model). Provenance, integrity, access control, and lineage are AI-security controls — not just governance nice-to-haves.

3.3 · Computing Power

  • Hardware — GPUs/TPUs provide the parallelism deep learning needs; CPUs orchestrate.
  • Training vs. inference — training is expensive and periodic; inference is cheaper per call but runs constantly at scale.
  • Distributed training — large models split across many accelerators.
  • Scaling trend — capability has grown roughly with more data + more parameters + more compute.
Security Lens

Compute is both a DoS target and a supply-chain concern. LLM apps face unbounded resource consumption ("denial of wallet" — driving up inference cost) and model DoS via expensive queries. The training infra, GPU clusters, MLOps tooling, and dependencies form a software supply chain needing the same hygiene as any CI/CD system.

Section 4Introduction to Machine Learning

4.1 · What is Machine Learning?

Machine Learning (ML) is the subset of AI where systems learn patterns from data to improve at a task, without being explicitly programmed for every rule. A common working definition (paraphrasing Tom Mitchell): a program learns from experience E on a task T, measured by performance P, if its performance on T improves with E. The term is usually credited to Arthur Samuel (1959).

4.2 · Differences between AI, ML, and DL

These are nested subsets, not competitors.

AIMLDeep Learning
ScopeBroadest — includes rule-based systemsSubset of AI that learns from dataSubset of ML using deep neural nets
Feature engineeringN/A or manualOften manualAutomatic (learned representations)
Data appetiteVariesModerateLarge
InterpretabilityHigh (symbolic)ModerateLow (opaque)

4.3 · Key ML Concepts

  • Features & labels — features are input variables; the label is the target you predict.
  • Train / validation / test split — train to learn, validate to tune, test to honestly measure. Test data stays unseen until the end.
  • Overfitting vs. underfitting — overfitting memorizes training data (incl. noise) and fails on new data (high variance); underfitting is too simple to capture the pattern (high bias). The bias–variance tradeoff balances them.
  • Parameters vs. hyperparameters — parameters are learned (weights); hyperparameters are chosen by you (learning rate, tree depth, layer count).
  • Loss + gradient descent — loss quantifies "how wrong"; gradient descent iteratively adjusts parameters to reduce it.
  • Regularization — penalize complexity to curb overfitting.

Precision vs. recall — essential for security work

Predicted PositivePredicted Negative
Actually PositiveTrue Positive (TP)False Negative (FN)
Actually NegativeFalse Positive (FP)True Negative (TN)

Precision = TP / (TP + FP) — of what I flagged, how much was real? (controls alert fatigue)
Recall = TP / (TP + FN) — of all real threats, how many did I catch? (controls misses)
F1 = harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Security Lens

In detection engineering, the precision/recall balance is the product. High recall + low precision buries the SOC; high precision + low recall misses intrusions. Overfitting has a security twist too: an overfit model memorizes training data, enabling membership inference and data-extraction — a privacy leak, not just an accuracy problem.

Section 5Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)

An LLM's knowledge is frozen at training time; on its own it can't access private/current data and may hallucinate. RAG retrieves relevant external information at query time and feeds it into the model's context so the answer is grounded — without retraining.

Step by step: ingest & chunk documents → embed each chunk into a vector (similar meaning → nearby vectors) → store in a vector database (FAISS, Pinecone, Milvus, Weaviate, Chroma, pgvector) → at query time retrieve the top-k most similar chunks → augment the prompt → generate a grounded answer, ideally with citations. Benefits: current/private knowledge without retraining, less hallucination, source attribution, cheap updates.

Security Lens — RAG concentrates several LLM risks
  • Indirect prompt injection — retrieved documents become part of the prompt, so an attacker who can plant text in a source (wiki, shared doc, crawled site) can smuggle instructions. Instruction and data share one channel.
  • Knowledge-base / retrieval poisoning — corrupt the indexed content and you corrupt every downstream answer.
  • Broken access control — if retrieval doesn't enforce the user's permissions, RAG becomes a confused deputy leaking documents. Authorize retrieval per identity, not just at the app boundary.
  • Embedding weaknesses — embeddings can leak source text (inversion); poorly isolated stores risk cross-tenant leakage.

Treat retrieved content as untrusted input: isolate instructions from data, enforce least privilege on the index, validate and attribute outputs.

Section 6Basics of Deep Learning

6.1 · What is Deep Learning?

Deep Learning (DL) is the subset of ML that uses neural networks with many layers. Its superpower is representation learning: instead of humans hand-crafting features, the network learns hierarchical features automatically — simple patterns in early layers combining into complex concepts deeper in. The cost: it's data-hungry, compute-hungry, and opaque.

6.2 · Introduction to Neural Networks

The single neuron

Each neuron multiplies inputs by weights, adds a bias, then passes the sum through a non-linear activation function.

The network & how it learns

Security Lens

The learned decision boundary is high-dimensional and non-intuitive — which is exactly why adversarial examples work. Attackers use the model's own gradients (e.g., FGSM/PGD) to compute tiny perturbations that push an input across the boundary while looking unchanged. White-box (gradient) access makes this easy; black-box attacks work via queries. Defenses (adversarial training, input validation, monitoring) help but none are complete.

6.3 · Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)

CNNs are neural networks specialized for grid-like data, above all images. They exploit spatial structure instead of wiring every pixel to every neuron.

Three signature operations: convolution (small filters/kernels slide over the image producing feature maps — with parameter sharing and translation invariance), pooling (downsamples, keeping strongest signals and adding robustness to shifts), and fully connected layers (produce the final classification).

Security Lens
  • Adversarial patches/perturbations — physical stickers or patterns that fool detectors (e.g., misclassifying a stop sign).
  • Backdoor/trojan attacks — a CNN trained on poisoned data behaves normally until it sees a hidden trigger, then outputs the attacker's target. Especially dangerous with pretrained models from untrusted sources.
  • Model extraction & inversion — cloning a deployed vision model or reconstructing training images.

Provenance of both data and pretrained weights is the strongest early control.

Appendix BGoverning Frameworks

Use these as the control catalogs and shared vocabulary of AI security. Standards evolve — confirm the current version/list on the official source before citing specific item numbers.

  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) — organizes AI risk around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Best for program-level structure.
  • MITRE ATLAS (Adversarial Threat Landscape for AI Systems) — an ATT&CK-style knowledge base of real-world adversarial-ML tactics and techniques. Best for threat modeling and detection mapping.
  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications — the go-to list of LLM-specific risks, including prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, supply chain, data & model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, and vector/embedding weaknesses. Best for application-level review.
